Standing over your decisions

~2 min read

Whether you are a lecturer new to the sector, a practitioner who has recently moved from industry, or an experienced teacher stepping into a programme leadership role, at some point you will be asked to sit on a board, sign off a result, or defend a grade. This session is about doing that with confidence.

By the end of this unit you should be able to:

  • Explain why quality assurance and academic oversight matter in Irish higher education, and how they operate at Levels 7, 8 and 9 of the National Framework of Qualifications
  • Participate effectively in Programme Boards and Exam Boards, and work productively with an external examiner
  • Justify academic judgements transparently, and recognise and avoid common QA pitfalls

Throughout, I will ask you to bring your own professional experience into play. If you have worked in industry, you already know something about audit, compliance, peer review and professional standards. That knowledge is directly transferable here, so use it.

Section 2 of 7

Why quality assurance matters in Irish higher education

~5 min read

Quality assurance can sound like administration, but it is really about a promise.

When we award a qualification, we are telling learners, employers, professional bodies and society that this person has achieved certain knowledge, skills and competence. Academic oversight is how we keep that promise credible.

In Ireland, that promise operates within a clear legal and policy framework. The Qualifications and Quality Assurance (Education and Training) Act 2012, as amended in 2019, established Quality and Qualifications Ireland, QQI, and gave it responsibility for the National Framework of Qualifications and for the external review of quality assurance in providers.

QQI's Core Statutory Quality Assurance Guidelines set out expectations for areas such as governance, programme design and approval, assessment of learners, and staff recruitment and development. These sit alongside topic-specific guidelines, for example on blended learning, and sector-specific guidance. Across Europe, the same principles reflect the Standards and Guidelines for Quality Assurance in the European Higher Education Area, usually called the ESG.

At SETU, Academic Council holds primary responsibility for the quality of programmes, the learning environment and assessment leading to awards, with oversight from Governing Body. The Regulations you will use in this session are an expression of that responsibility.

In the Regulations: section 1. Legal anchor: Technological Universities Act 2018, section 17(1). The Regulations draw on QQI's Assessment and Standards (revised 2022), QQI's Core Statutory Quality Assurance Guidelines (2016), the Joint-Sectoral Protocol on inclusion of qualifications in the NFQ (2022), the ESG (2015) and the SETU Quality Framework.

What does “quality” mean?

Harvey and Green, in their influential 1993 paper, argued that quality is not one thing. It can mean exceptional, perfection or consistency, fitness for purpose, value for money, or transformation of the learner.

Ireland's approach leans towards fitness for purpose and enhancement. Quality assurance is expected to protect standards, and it is also expected to help us improve. The National Forum for the Enhancement of Teaching and Learning has consistently framed it that way, emphasising a culture of enhancement rather than compliance alone.

Section 3 of 7

Participating in Programme Boards and Exam Boards

~7 min read

Now to the places where QA actually happens. The two you are most likely to encounter are the Programme Board and the Exam Board. Titles and structures vary between institutions, so always check your own institution's academic regulations, but the functions are broadly similar.

Programme Boards

A Programme Board, sometimes called a Course Board, takes a view of the programme as a whole. It is concerned with the learner experience, the coherence of the curriculum, alignment between module learning outcomes and programme learning outcomes, and the health of the programme over time. It typically reviews learner feedback, progression and retention data, external examiner reports, and proposed modifications.

In SETU's Regulations the Programme Board is the primary management unit for a programme. It reports to the Faculty Management Team and is ultimately accountable to Academic Council Regulations section 7.4.4–7.4.5. Membership is not ceremonial: the Regulations list its responsibilities, from monitoring the education standard to reviewing external examiner reports Regulations section 7.4.7.

Programme Boards: what the Regulations say

At minimum: the Programme Leader, the Head of Department, all academic staff involved in delivery, and student representatives.

At least once every semester. The agenda is circulated in advance with an Any Other Business item, and minutes are retained by the Faculty. Reserved items, such as individual student results, can exclude student representatives.

Declare them and, where necessary, step out of the relevant discussion.

Publish an assessment strategy cross-referenced to learning outcomes and national standards, and prepare and review an assessment schedule to avoid over-assessment.

An Annual Programme Board Report to Academic Council.

A useful principle here is constructive alignment, from John Biggs (1996): learning outcomes, teaching activities and assessment should all point at the same thing. When you sit on a Programme Board, constructive alignment is your best diagnostic tool.

A question to carry in. If a module claims to develop critical evaluation, ask where it is taught, and ask where it is assessed.

Closing the loop

  1. Receive the evidence. Data, feedback, the external examiner report.
  2. Identify themes and risks.
  3. Agree actions with owners and deadlines.
  4. Record the decision and the rationale.
  5. Close the loop at the next meeting.

That final step, closing the loop, is where many programmes fall down. QQI's guidelines place considerable emphasis on this: collecting feedback matters less than demonstrably acting on it and telling stakeholders what changed. Learners and external examiners notice quickly when their input vanishes into a void.

The Regulations give the loop a concrete shape. The Annual Programme Board Report must cover progress on earlier issues, actions on external examiner and student feedback, actions on input from external bodies and industry, recruitment, performance, progression and retention, and plans for development Regulations section 7.8.3.2. Faculties then send summaries to Academic Council Regulations section 7.8.4.

Exam Boards

An Exam Board confirms results and makes decisions about progression, awards and classifications. Its purpose is to ensure that assessment outcomes are fair, consistent, and in line with the institution's regulations. Typically, it will consider module results, any moderation or second-marking outcomes, mitigating circumstances, academic integrity cases, and the external examiner's views on standards.

How an Exam Board differs from a Programme Board. A Programme Board looks forward, towards improvement. An Exam Board looks at specific outcomes for specific learners, so confidentiality, consistency and procedural fairness come first.

SETU's Regulations are specific about how an Exam Board works. It meets after each examination session, chaired by the Head of Faculty, and it confirms module marks, progression and achievement Regulations section 8.10.6 Regulations section 8.10.12. Decisions are by majority with a casting vote for the Chair, and deliberations are strictly confidential Regulations section 8.10.10 Regulations section 8.10.17.

Exam Board pathway

  1. Internal Examiners grade using clear marking schemes Regulations section 8.10.4.
  2. Marks and moderation evidence go to the Board.
  3. The Board confirms that processes followed Academic Council regulations, approves marks, considers whether mark ranges are appropriate and moderates where appropriate, and agrees re-assessment Regulations section 8.10.9.
  4. The broadsheet is approved and signed Regulations section 8.10.18–8.10.19.
  5. Results go to the Vice President for Academic Affairs, Teaching and Learning, then to Academic Council for ratification Regulations section 8.10.18.

What would you do?

Scenario · at the Exam Board

You are at an Exam Board. A Level 8 module shows a mean mark fifteen points higher than the programme average. The module coordinator says the cohort was exceptionally strong. What do you do?

Anomalies warrant inquiry rather than assumptions. Notice what the best option has in common with good practice in any profession: you do not assume anything is wrong, and you do not accept explanations without evidence. You ask for the evidence.

The Regulations back this up. A module whose grade distribution is persistently and significantly inconsistent with others warrants investigation, and systematic anomalies are reported to the Exam Board and notified to Academic Council Regulations section 8.10.16. External Examiners are also expected to contribute to the Board's view on grade distributions Regulations section 8.10.15.

Practical tips for taking part

  1. Prepare. Read the papers before the meeting and flag questions in advance.
  2. Speak up when something does not make sense. New staff sometimes assume that everyone else understands and stay silent, but a fresh perspective is often exactly what a board needs.
  3. Make sure your contribution is recorded in the minutes, because the minutes are your evidence trail.
  4. Separate the person from the data. Boards work best when discussion focuses on patterns and evidence rather than on individual blame.

Section 4 of 7

The role of the external examiner

~5 min read

External examining is one of the longest-standing mechanisms in Irish and UK higher education. An external examiner is an independent, appropriately qualified academic or professional from outside your institution, appointed to give an impartial view on whether standards are appropriate and whether assessment is fair and consistently applied.

QQI's guidelines on assessment and standards expect providers to use external expertise in this way, and institutional regulations set out the details.

An external examiner usually reviews draft assessment briefs and exam papers, samples learners' work across the mark range, and attends or contributes to the Exam Board. They then write a report, which is shared with the Programme Board and often with learners, and which requires a formal response from the programme team.

In SETU, External Examiners are appointed by the University, are independent of it, and are involved at relevant stages of assessment Regulations section 8.3.15. They submit a standardised report on the curriculum, the assessment, the standard of student work and the fairness and consistency of assessment practice Regulations section 7.8.2.2. The Programme Board considers each report before a response goes back on every recommendation Regulations section 7.8.2.4.

Your assessment and the External Examiner

  1. Internal Examiners prepare assessments and marking schemes and consult the External Examiner on drafting Regulations section 8.2.2(i) Regulations section 8.2.3(i).
  2. All continuous assessment briefs and marking schemes are seen by External Examiners Regulations section 8.2.2(iii).
  3. Marks and evidence are made available to them Regulations section 8.2.2(iv).
  4. They contribute at the Exam Board Regulations section 8.10.15.
  5. Their report feeds all quality assurance procedures, and action is taken and reported where recommendations are made Regulations section 7.8.2.3.

Detailed duties and timelines. Programme Boards can also recommend External Examiners for consideration Regulations section 7.4.7.

What externals do

Comment on standards, comparability with similar programmes, assessment design, and consistency of marking.

What externals don't do

Remark individual scripts, reassign marks unilaterally, or act as a replacement for internal moderation.

What they need from you

Clear materials, representative samples, timely access, open responses.

A critical friend, not an inspector

The most common misunderstanding is to treat the external examiner as an inspector to be impressed. A more productive view treats them as a critical friend. They have comparative knowledge of other institutions and a fresh pair of eyes, and that is valuable.

At Level 9, particularly, external examiners may carry real weight in judging the standard of a dissertation or research project, and at Level 7 they may offer helpful perspectives on whether practical competence is being assessed to a professionally credible standard.

How should you work with them?

  • Be organised and transparent. Provide what they need in good time, including marking criteria and examples of how they were applied.
  • Respond to every point in the report, even where you disagree, and explain your reasoning.
  • Then use the report: the best programme teams treat it as a free source of enhancement ideas.

The Regulations set the same expectation. Internal Examiners must consider every suggestion, criticism, deletion, addition and amendment an External Examiner proposes, and make sure the External Examiner has signed or otherwise confirmed the marking sheets Regulations section 8.2.3(ii) Regulations section 8.2.3(v).

Section 5 of 7

Academic judgement and decision-making

~5 min read

This section is the heart of the session. Every mark, grade and progression decision rests on academic judgement.

We like to think of marking as objective, but the research tells a more nuanced story. D. Royce Sadler (2010), whose work on standards and assessment is widely cited, has argued that academic judgement relies partly on tacit knowledge built through experience, and that explicit criteria alone cannot fully capture what an expert marker recognises as quality. Sue Bloxham (2007) and colleagues have shown that markers can interpret the same criteria differently and rely on their own internalised standards.

This is not a failure. It is a feature of expert judgement, but it means we must work deliberately to make judgement reliable, transparent and defensible.

The National Forum's work on assessment, including its guidance on the principles of assessment and its emphasis on assessment for, as and of learning, reminds us that sound judgement starts with sound assessment design. If the task and the criteria are well aligned with the learning outcomes, judgement becomes more straightforward.

SETU's Regulations set the ground rules for judgement. Assessment must measure achievement of learning outcomes, and every module learning outcome must be assessed summatively Regulations section 8.3.11 Regulations section 8.3.13. Criteria and grading standards must reach students before the assessment, and grading is done by the Internal Examiner using clear, consistent and transparent marking schemes Regulations section 8.3.10 Regulations section 8.10.4.

Judgement in the Regulations: before, during, after

  • Criteria and marking scheme shared with students Regulations section 8.3.10 Regulations section 8.3.12.
  • Assessment brief with due date, submission method and confirmation of submission Regulations section 8.7.2.
  • Marking scheme applied consistently Regulations section 8.10.4.
  • For group work, individual contribution assessed, with a viva voce available to verify it Regulations section 8.3.17.
  • Academic integrity concerns handled under the Student Academic Misconduct Policy and Disciplinary Procedure Regulations section 8.9.4 Regulations section 8.9.8. Poor academic practice is not an academic offence Regulations section 8.9.9.
  • Timely feedback that supports improvement Regulations section 8.13.1.
  • Students may apply to view marked summative work after results issue Regulations section 8.13.3.
  • Academic appeals follow the university's process Regulations section 8.14.

Four habits of defensible academic judgement

  1. Anchor to learning outcomes and the NFQ level indicators.
  2. Use the criteria and rubric consistently.
  3. Calibrate with colleagues, through moderation, second marking and benchmark samples.
  4. Record your reasoning so another person could follow it.

Three questions to ask when you award a mark. What evidence in the learner's work led me to this mark? How does that evidence map to the learning outcomes and the criteria? And would a colleague, given the same work and criteria, reach a similar conclusion? If you can answer yes to all three, you can stand over your decision.

Now consider the level

A Level 8 assignment expects critical analysis, so a well-organised description of the literature, however polished, should not earn top marks. At Level 9, you should look for synthesis, originality or a clearly argued contribution. At Level 7, you are more likely to be assessing accurate application of established methods.

The same assessment words, such as “analyse” or “evaluate”, can mean very different things at different levels, which is why the NFQ level indicators matter.

    Common QA pitfalls and how to avoid them

    ~4 min read

    Let's finish the content with some practical wisdom: the pitfalls that catch even experienced staff.

    The pitfall. Learning outcomes, activities and assessment do not match.

    How to avoid it. Map every outcome to an assessment and a teaching activity, and look for gaps.

    The pitfall. Criteria are broad, or markers do not apply them consistently.

    How to avoid it. Use clear rubrics with level-appropriate descriptors, and calibrate with colleagues before marking.

    The pitfall. Decisions are made but not recorded, so they cannot be justified later.

    How to avoid it. Keep brief, dated records of moderation, rationales and board decisions.

    The pitfall. Feedback from learners and external examiners is collected but not acted upon.

    How to avoid it. Keep an action log and report back on outcomes.

    The pitfall. Teams complete forms but never discuss what they reveal.

    How to avoid it. Use QA data to ask real questions about teaching and learning, in the spirit of the National Forum's enhancement-led approach.

    Two further traps

    Inconsistency between levels. Applying Level 8 expectations to a Level 7 cohort, or vice versa, creates unfair outcomes.

    Isolation. Staff who make decisions alone, without moderation or peer conversation, are much more likely to drift from the standard without noticing it.

    Four more, from the 2026-27 Regulations

    The pitfall. Too many or badly timed assessments overload learners.

    How to avoid it. Use the 5-credit norms of a 2-hour exam or a 2,000–4,000 word assignment, and review volume and timing against an assessment schedule Regulations section 8.3.6 Regulations section 8.3.7 Regulations section 8.4.3.

    The pitfall. Marks are adjusted informally, or documents arrive late.

    How to avoid it. Marks cannot be adjusted. Follow the deferral and re-sit route and the five-day documentation rule Regulations section 8.8.3 Regulations section 8.8.4.

    The pitfall. Board decisions are discussed outside the Board, or conflicts go undeclared.

    How to avoid it. Treat deliberations as strictly confidential and declare conflicts of interest Regulations section 8.10.17 Regulations section 7.4.9.

    The pitfall. ADF and SAM documents differ for exam rules, external examining and appeals.

    How to avoid it. Check which system your programme sits under (section 3).

    Summary and reflective activity

    ~2 min read

    Quality assurance in Irish higher education is a shared responsibility within a framework set by legislation, QQI guidelines, the ESG, and SETU — and it exists to protect standards and to enhance learning.
    Shared responsibility
    A framework set by legislation, QQI guidelines, the ESG and SETU, which exists to protect standards and to enhance learning.
    Two kinds of board
    Programme Boards look forward at the programme. Exam Boards look carefully at outcomes for individual learners.
    Critical friends
    External examiners add an independent, comparative perspective.
    Defensible judgement
    Academic judgement must always be evidence-based, calibrated with colleagues, and documented so that you can stand over it.

    In SETU terms, know three parts of the 2026-27 Regulations well: sections 7.4 and 7.8 for Programme Boards and external examiner reporting, section 8 for assessment and Exam Boards, and section 10 for classification of awards.

    Remember too that your experience outside academia is an asset. The habits of audit, evidence, peer review and professional accountability that you have developed are exactly what good QA depends on.
